# Observation 3 — Two repositories decided one world, and nothing coordinated it

C3 predicted that intention cannot meet before knowledge does, and both halves of it hold. The first
half is the one worth stating first, because it is the surprising one.

**Two repositories, founded independently, decided the same world.** Same identity, compared as
identities and not as answers:

```text
left.worlds[0]  ==  right.worlds[0]      the base: the fund, and nothing proposed against it
left.worlds[1]  !=  right.worlds[1]      and what each went on to intend is not one world
```

Neither was asked. A `Thesis` is identified by its parent, its cut and its selection; both sides had no
parent, resolved the same cut at the same instant, and selected the same commitment — which is the same
commitment because a `CommitmentId` is derived from content. So the agreement is not a merge, an
exchange or a coincidence of rendering. It is what content addressing does when two records say the
same thing.

## And the condition, measured at the cut rather than at the decision

The second half is what keeps the first from being magic. A `KnowledgeCut` is
`(known_at, event_head)`, and the head is **resolved against the Event chain that stood**. So the same
decision, taken over knowledge that differs by one Event, produces a different world:

```text
                        known_at        event_head        world
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
with the Event          2026-01-10      Some(…)           A
without it              2026-01-10      None              B        A != B
```

The instant named is identical. The selection is identical. The decision, as a record, is identical in
everything a caller wrote down. What moved is the knowledge underneath, and the world's identity moved
with it — which is the engine's own answer to *a cut is not reproducible from its date alone*, arriving
here as the mechanism that makes agreement mean something.

> **Two repositories agree about a world exactly when they agreed about the knowledge under it.**

So the free agreement is conditional on the very thing Observation 1 measured a refusal about, and the
tension the protocol pre-registered resolves in favour of the record: nothing agrees by accident.

## Which is also why the refusal is not an oversight

It would be tempting, holding Observation 2, to say the merge should be more permissive — two
repositories share a derivable base, so why refuse them. This is the answer. Every standing decision
names the entries that stood when it was taken, and a world's identity is derived from a cut that
resolves against them. A merge that admitted a journal it had not agreed about would be building worlds
whose identities the record cannot reproduce, which is not a meeting. It is two records losing the
property that made them comparable in the first place.

## What it does not say

That agreement about knowledge is *enough*. Observation 4 measures the other direction of the arrow,
and it does not hold: two repositories can share a whole journal and still agree about only one world.
