# The account, and how it got here

You asked me to explain why the account is heading below its floor. It is, by 20, and the
day it happens is 20 January. Nothing has gone wrong yet — no money has left the account,
nothing is late, and every one of the four ways out is still open. What follows is the
whole trail, including the part where the judgment was mine and it was wrong.

## Where the account actually stands

There is 100 in the account. That is not an estimate: it is the one payment that has
actually happened — money received and confirmed settled on 2 January. Nothing has been
paid out since.

Against that 100 stand two promises the house has made, both still outstanding:

- **30, due 14 January**
- **90, due 20 January**

That is 120 promised against 100 held. On 14 January the 30 goes out and the account sits
at 70 — comfortable. On 20 January the 90 goes out and the account is at **-20**. That is
the breach, and it is the only one: the 30 is not the problem, and no amount of reordering
makes it one.

I checked that conclusion three different ways — assuming everything simply settles
eventually, assuming each promise is paid on the day it falls due, and assuming the worst
ordering of anything falling on the same day. All three give the same number, -20. There
is no arrangement of these two payments that fits inside 100.

## How it got here, day by day

**2 January.** 100 arrives and is confirmed. This is the only money the account has ever
actually held.

**6 January.** The house intends to spend 120, due 8 January. I put that intention into the
plan and asked whether the plan held. It did not: 100 in, 120 out, the account ends at -20.
The intention was **withdrawn the same day it was made**, before a single unit of money
moved.

That is the first thing I would ask you to notice. The check is not something run at the
end of the month over what already happened; it runs against the whole plan the moment an
intention enters it. It caught a 120 that would have overdrawn the account by 20 and it
caught it on the day, not on the due date two days later.

The second thing to notice is what happened to that withdrawn 120. It was not deleted. It
is still on the record, marked cancelled, which is precisely why I can show you this
near-miss instead of asking you to take my word that the account has been watched. Nothing
in this account has ever been erased; every version of the plan is still there, each one
naming the one it came from, so this story cannot be quietly rewritten after the fact —
including by me.

**6 January, later.** With the 120 gone, the house intends to spend 30, due 14 January. I
put it into the plan and asked again: 100 in, 30 out, 70 left. It held. This is the
promise that is still outstanding and still fine.

**9 January.** The house intends to spend 90, due 20 January.

**12 January.** The plan is brought forward to the 12th and the 90 is added to it. The
answer comes back immediately: **-20 on 20 January**. That is where we are.

## Why the 90 is the mistake, and why it is mine

Every step above was sound on its own terms, and that is exactly the trap. The 30 was fine
— it left 70. The 90 would also have been fine on its own — it would have left 10. What
does not fit is the two of them together against a single 100 that has to cover both.

The check is not fooled by that, because it always asks about the whole plan rather than
about the newest item, which is why it fired the moment the 90 joined the other two. But
firing is not preventing. I committed the house to the 90 while the 30 was outstanding and
before any money had been secured to cover it. If that 90 was taken on in the expectation
of something coming in, **that expectation was never written down** — and something not
written down cannot be counted. The account has a way to record money expected in; it was
available on the day and it was not used. That is the failure, and it is mine, not the
counterparty's and not the market's.

I can be exact about the last point, and it matters for how you read the rest. Every change
to this plan was a decision by the house. Nothing was forced on it from outside: no
counterparty defaulted, nothing arrived late, no fact showed up that anyone had to absorb.
I know this because the system keeps the two apart on purpose — changing what the house
intends and taking in what the world has done are separate acts that leave separate marks.
Across this entire history, nothing ever entered the plan that the house had not chosen to
put there, and the only two facts ever recorded against it are the 100 arriving and the
house's own withdrawal of the 120. The shortfall is not bad luck. It is two decisions, made three days apart, that were each defensible and are
not defensible together.

**One process failure worth naming.** The 90 was decided on the 9th, but the plan on the
table at that moment was dated the 6th, and a plan dated the 6th cannot speak about a
decision made on the 9th. It had to be brought forward to the 12th before the 90 could be
added to it — and the warning came with it. Had I brought the plan forward on the 9th, when
the decision was made, you would have had this conversation three days earlier with three
more days to act. Nothing was hidden in that window, but nothing was asked in it either.
Going forward, a decision should join the plan on the day it is made.

## What is still true in our favour

None of this has cost anything yet, and that is not luck either — it is the direct result
of the discipline above.

- **No money has moved.** The 100 is intact. Both outstanding promises are unpaid.
- **Nothing is late.** As of the 12th, both promises are inside their deadlines.
- **The 30 is not at risk.** Paying it on the 14th leaves 70, which covers nothing that
  comes after but breaches nothing either.
- **We have until the 20th** — over a week — and the problem is a single promise, not a
  pattern.

## The four ways out

I tested each of these against the plan the same way everything else here was tested. All
four clear the floor under all three ways of asking:

1. **Withdraw the 90.** The account ends at 70. Cleanest, and costs the house whatever the
   90 was for.
2. **Withdraw the 30 instead.** The account ends at 10. It clears, but it leaves almost no
   margin, and I would not recommend trading a comfortable promise for a tight one.
3. **Secure 20 coming in, by 19 January.** Both promises are kept and the account lands at
   exactly 0. Note the word *exactly*: 20 is the minimum that works, so anything that
   slips leaves us where we are now. If we go this way I would want more than 20 and I
   would want it recorded the day it is agreed, not the day it arrives.
4. **Reduce the 90 to 70.** Both counterparties keep something and the account lands at 0.
   This one needs the other side's agreement, which the first two do not.

Which of these is right is not a judgment I should make on your behalf — it depends on what
the 90 was for and on what the house's word is worth to that counterparty. That is why I am
bringing you four options rather than a decision.

What I will say plainly: the process caught this before it cost anything, twice — once on
the 6th, when it stopped a 120 that would have overdrawn the account, and once on the 12th,
when it caught the 30-and-90 that I had put there. The first time the answer changed my
mind in time. The second time it is a warning you are reading eight days before the money
is due. That the shortfall exists at all is my error. That you are hearing about it now,
with the account still whole and every option still open, is the system doing what it was
built to do.
